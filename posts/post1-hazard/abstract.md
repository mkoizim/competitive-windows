Anticipating Competitive Window Length: A Hazard Model Analysis of FanGraphs Opening Day Playoff Odds

Front office strategy is heavily influenced by expectations of “competitive windows,” during which the Major League team will be positioned to contend for a postseason berth. Prior research on competitive window length is based on observed results (Jordan, Baseball Research Journal, 2018; Reinhart, The Hardball Times, 2018). However, no known research exists into MLB window length based on predicted outcomes. Because organizations make player personnel decisions based on predictions, assessing the probability of a window remaining open is of critical strategic importance.

The methodology is an exploratory hazard model analysis of window length using 2014–2026 Opening Day FanGraphs Playoff Odds. A berth-relative model deems a team to be in a window if it ranks within its league’s available postseason berths by Opening Day playoff odds. A fixed-rank model deems a team to be in a window when it ranks in the top 6 out of the 15 in its league by Opening Day playoff odds (variations setting the threshold from top 3 to top 9 are robustness checks). Windows are defined to be closed once the team is projected below the threshold on the next Opening Day. Windows open in 2014 are excluded, as their true age is not available in the data. All models are discrete-time logistic hazard regressions of team-seasons on current window age, Opening Day payroll, postseason format, and “cushion” (wins per 162 above the first team below the window threshold, as projected on that season’s Opening Day).

Results from the berth-relative definition (36 windows, 28 closings) indicate 56% of newly opened windows survived after two seasons; 19% survived after four. The hazard model showed each projected win in a team’s cushion lowered the odds of its window closing (odds ratio per projected win 0.67; 95% CI 0.53–0.84; p=0.0017). Among windows with the same Opening Day cushion, older windows were more likely to close (odds ratio 2.60 per doubling of window age; 95% CI 1.21–5.58; p=0.016). Without cushion in the model, age was not a significant predictor of closing (p=0.65). Cushion also predicted actually missing the next postseason (odds ratio 0.83; 95% CI 0.73–0.94; p=0.0047).

At the fixed line (top-six teams), cushion lowered the odds of a window closing similarly (odds ratio 0.72; 95% CI 0.59–0.89; p=0.0033) and was significant in all robustness variations except top-four. Window age was not statistically significant at the top-six threshold (p=0.30); it was significant only at the top-five and top-eight robustness variations.

Neither Opening Day payroll nor postseason format was statistically significant in any model.

This research contributes the first known hazard model of projected competitive windows, using the lens through which front offices must approach decisions: predicted outcomes. It will also provide a foundation for future analyses into organizational decisions to build for future seasons versus capitalizing on an open window, and how such decisions may impact the length of a window.
